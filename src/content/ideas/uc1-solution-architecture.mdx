<p class="moved">The delivery pattern is presented in <a href="/sessions/proof-point-delivery/">Session 1</a>, and the NBA hand-off, the content model and the content reference in <a href="/sessions/nba-hand-off/">Session 2</a>. <Section n="01" id="resolution-layer" title="The resolution layer" nav="Resolution layer">
  <div data-origin="uc1:position:4" class="decide risk">
    <h4><span class="stamp">new</span>A thin resolution service sits between Contentful and the channels</h4>
    <p>Channels do not call the Contentful delivery API directly. A small stateless service — the <strong>Content Resolution API</strong> — parses the reference, serves an immutable published snapshot, applies and <em>reports</em> translation fallback, composes the message, and shapes the channel payload.</p>
    <p>This is the one net-new component in the design, and it needs saying plainly: the delivery API alone cannot satisfy three of the stated requirements. It serves only the latest published version, so "exactly the same content" breaks the moment an author republishes mid-campaign. Its locale fallback is silent, so a consumer cannot report on it. And it will not survive a burst of concurrent fetches after a newsletter send without a cache in front of it. If the guarantee is that every channel gets identical content, that guarantee has to be enforced in one place — otherwise the pilot proves consistency of input, not of output.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:position:6" class="decide risk">
    <h4><span class="stamp">deferred</span>Grammatical agreement is out of POC scope, and has to be solved before production</h4>
    <p>For the POC, copy is plain text with named variables and a straight substitution at resolution time. Authors write one sentence per locale with no syntax beyond the variable itself. Number formatting follows the locale — thousands separators and decimal marks — but nothing in the sentence adapts to the value.</p>
    <p><strong>What this knowingly leaves broken.</strong> A value of 1 produces "seen 1 times". German and Thai degrade the same way. Arabic is worse: six plural categories, with the noun and any surrounding adjective changing alongside the number, so one sentence cannot be correct across the range. Ordinals and any gendered noun driven by the variable have the same problem.</p>
    <p><strong>Why it is acceptable here and nowhere else.</strong> The POC tests whether one authored source reaches every channel consistently, translated and versioned. Substitution proves that. But CMS-47 — composing the metric into a grammatically correct, locale-appropriate sentence — is one of the capabilities EG is evaluating, and the one they describe as a capability gain rather than an efficiency gain. Deferring it means that capability is <strong>demonstrated in principle, not proven</strong>, and the readout should say so rather than let it pass unremarked.</p>
    <p><strong>What full implementation needs.</strong> A plural-aware message format — ICU is the standard and every translation tool already parses it — with authoring that keeps the syntax away from the author: a plain source sentence expanded into plural categories by a generation step, approved once, and stored in the machine-readable form. Two things drive the cost: whether the real parameter types are counts only or also currency, dates and ordinals, and whether Arabic stays in the locale set. Four of the five pilot locales need at most two forms; Arabic alone accounts for most of the work.</p>
  </div>

<h3>Alternatives considered</h3>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:0" class="decide risk">
    <h4>Tone and channel as a single dimension</h4>
    <p>The argument for it is real: the channel does determine the voice of a communication, so a separate tone dimension could look redundant. Collapsing the two would simplify the model and cut the variant count by roughly a third.</p>
    <p>Not adopted, because it works against the source document's thesis rather than simplifying it. Step 5 states that the CRM shows the Market Manager the same tone of voice the partner already received, and the partner walkthrough presents persistent, persona-matched framing as the benefit being tested. If tone is a property of the channel, a partner's voice changes every time they move between the email, the portal and a call — the drift the pilot exists to eliminate.</p>
    <p>It would also cost three capability results at once: CMS-19 loses half its pass condition because tone stops being decisioning metadata, CMS-31's criterion collapses from segment plus tone to segment alone, and CMS-06 becomes trivially satisfiable since no cross-channel constant remains.</p>
    <p>The observation underneath it is kept: a channel does impose something — a shape, and for CRM a different reader — and both are modelled, as the channel profile and the variant's briefing, without a second voice. Should EG's operating model genuinely treat tone as channel-determined, the change is one field plus a re-scoping of those three capabilities — an explicit client decision, which is why it is raised as a question rather than assumed either way.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:1" class="decide risk">
    <h4>Channels calling the delivery API directly, no resolution layer</h4>
    <p>Fewest moving parts and closest to the source document's description. Not taken, for three reasons: the delivery API serves only the current published version, so version pinning is impossible and a mid-campaign republish silently changes what was sent; locale fallback is silent, so no consumer can report on it; and every channel would have to implement message composition independently, which reintroduces exactly the drift the pilot exists to eliminate.</p>
    <p>If EG wants the minimal version anyway, it is viable for the CRM and VMC payload comparison — but not for SFMC, and not for anything claiming immutability.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:2" class="decide risk">
    <h4>One entry per partner, holding the partner's metric</h4>
    <p>The literal reading of the scale note. Not taken: it puts partner data in a content store, makes the entry count a function of the partner base, and makes every metric refresh a content operation. Nothing about it is a content-management problem.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:3" class="decide risk">
    <h4>Contentful resolving segment and tone to a variant</h4>
    <p>Fewer round trips and closer to how personalisation products work. Not taken because it contradicts the stated parameter that NBA decides, pulls a licensed personalisation capability onto the critical path, and makes the fetch non-deterministic and harder to cache. Worth revisiting after the pilot if EG wants decisioning closer to the content.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:4" class="decide risk">
    <h4>An opaque hashed content ID</h4>
    <p>Tidier on the wire. Not taken: an opaque token cannot be read in a log, a support ticket or an SFMC preview, and the traceability requirement is precisely about being able to answer "what was this" long after the send.</p>
  </div>

<Section n="02" id="authoring" title="Authoring flow">
<div data-origin="uc1:authoring:*">
  <p class="sec-note">Four phases, three human gates. Generation is automated; approval never is. Nothing reaches a channel without a named person having accepted it.</p>

  <div class="fig">
    <Fragment set:html={uc1Fragment3} />
  </div>
  <p class="cap">Figure 2 — Authoring flow. The design decision worth arguing about is in phase C.</p>

  <h3>Phase B — generated tone variants and briefings</h3>
  <p>An AI action runs against the approved base template and produces a draft variant per tone — partner copy and a briefing for the Market Manager — then checks each variant against every channel profile and flags the ones that need an override. The author sees a diff and accepts, edits or rejects. Two things make this safe enough to put in front of a client:</p>
  <ul class="tight">
    <li><strong>Placeholders are validated, not trusted.</strong> Generated copy is rejected automatically if it drops, renames or duplicates a variable declared on the proof point. A tone rewrite that loses <code>\{views\}</code> is a broken send, and it is the most likely generation failure.</li>
    <li><strong>Provenance is recorded.</strong> <code>generatedBy</code> and the approving user are stored on the entry, so any later review can separate machine-written copy from hand-written copy.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Availability of the AI actions feature on EG's plan needs confirming before this is committed. If it isn't available, the same flow runs through an external generation step writing drafts via the management API — more moving parts, same gates.</p>

  <h3>Phase C — the translation approval gate</h3>
  <p>This is where a platform constraint bites: publishing acts on the entry, so every locale goes live together, and a per-locale approval gate appears impossible.</p>
  <p>The way through is to treat the empty field as the gate. A translation lives in the translation workflow until a reviewer approves it, and only then is it written into the localised field. Unapproved locales stay empty, native fallback serves the source copy, and the resolver flags that a fallback happened. Publishing becomes safe at any moment because the entry never contains unapproved copy.</p>
  <p>The trade is that an unapproved locale is not "blocked" — it is served in English and reported as such. For a pilot testing whether partners get consistent content that is the better failure mode, and it turns a modelling problem into a reporting one. It should be stated explicitly to EG rather than discovered at the readout.</p>
</div>
</Section>


<Section n="03" id="validation" title="Validation plan">
<div data-origin="uc1:validation:*">
  <p class="sec-note">The seven points you listed, each with the evidence it produces and the condition that decides pass or fail. Every one is machine-checkable except the two where the judgement is genuinely editorial.</p>
  <div class="scroll">
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th style="width:20%">Validation point</th><th style="width:34%">How it's exercised</th><th>Pass condition</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Variants of the same content</strong></td>
        <td>Two proof points, three tones, two segments — twelve variants. Each fetched by its own reference and diffed against the others.</td>
        <td>Twelve variants resolve independently and correctly from two base proof points, with no duplicated base copy. Adding a thirteenth needs no content-type change.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Automatic generation for tone and channel</strong></td>
        <td>AI action generates tone variants from the approved base — partner copy and a briefing each — into draft state, then every variant is checked against the email, CRM and VMC channel profiles.</td>
        <td>Generated copy uses every variable declared on the proof point and no other; the placeholder check rejects any output that drops or renames one; the partner tone survives into every channel, including the CRM briefing; the author can accept, edit or reject without leaving the editor; provenance is recorded on the entry; the number of variants that needed a hand-authored override is reported — that count is the CMS-01 result.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Translation into five locales</strong></td>
        <td>Approved variants sent for translation into the four target locales; three returned and approved; one deliberately held back untranslated.</td>
        <td>Translated copy keeps every variable intact and correctly placed for the target language; the untranslated locale serves source copy with <code>fallbackApplied: true</code>; no other locale is affected; Arabic round-trips without corruption.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Approval workflows</strong></td>
        <td>Three gates exercised end to end — generated copy, per-locale translation, release — including one rejection at each gate.</td>
        <td>Nothing unapproved is ever retrievable through the resolution API; a rejection returns the item to draft without affecting published content; each approval records who and when.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Version registry</strong></td>
        <td>A published variant is fetched, then edited and republished, then fetched again by the original reference.</td>
        <td>The original reference returns the original copy, byte for byte. The new reference returns the new copy. Both are retrievable, and the diff between versions is inspectable with author and timestamp.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>SFMC integration</strong></td>
        <td>SFMC fetches by reference at send time against the future-state instance and renders into a preview or seed send.</td>
        <td>Content populates the message from a single call; timeout, unknown reference and retired version each produce a defined, non-breaking behaviour; measured latency and throughput ceiling are documented rather than assumed.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>API returns the published version by reference, variant and locale</strong></td>
        <td>The same reference requested across three channels and five locales, repeatedly, including after a republish.</td>
        <td><code>consistencyKey</code> is identical across channels for a given reference, version, locale and parameter set; repeated fetches are byte-identical; the response always states which locale was actually served.</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  </div>
  <p>Two judgements stay human and should be scheduled as such: whether a generated tone variant is <em>good</em>, and whether the translated copy reads naturally in each locale. Both need a named reviewer per locale, and neither is a test we can run on EG's behalf. Reviewers should be briefed that grammatical agreement with the variable is out of scope for this round, so they flag translation quality rather than re-reporting a known limitation.</p>
</div>
</Section>
