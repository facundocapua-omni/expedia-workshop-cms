<Section n="01" id="position" title="Six design positions, taken up front" nav="Design positions">
  <p data-origin="uc1:position:0" class="sec-note">Each of these answers one of the open questions. If EG disagrees with any of them the design changes shape, so they belong at the top of the conversation rather than buried in a component description.</p>

  <div data-origin="uc1:position:1" class="decide">
    <h4><span class="stamp">Q1</span>Contentful stores the message template, never the partner's number</h4>
    <p>A proof-point entry holds localised copy with <strong>named variables</strong> — not <code>"seen 47 times"</code> but <code>"Your property page was seen \{views\} times in the last \{windowDays\} days"</code>. The value arrives with NBA's decision and is substituted at resolution time.</p>
    <p>This is what keeps the content set at variant scale — dozens of entries — instead of partner scale. No entry anywhere holds a partner identifier or a partner's metric.</p>
    <p>Grammatical agreement between the value and the sentence around it is <strong>out of scope for the POC</strong>. See the <a href="/sessions/proof-point-delivery/#resolution-layer">deferred-scope note in Session 1</a>, because it is not a detail.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:position:2" class="decide">
    <h4><span class="stamp">Q2</span>NBA selects the variant; Contentful never decides</h4>
    <p>NBA reads a <strong>variant index</strong> published out of Contentful — every variant with its segment tags, tone, available channels and translated locales — and picks one. The content platform is asked for a specific thing by identifier and returns it. No audience rules, no conditional blocks, no personalisation engine inside the CMS.</p>
    <p>This matches the pilot parameters as written, keeps the personalisation licence out of the critical path, and makes the fetch deterministic and cacheable. The cost is a new obligation the source document doesn't mention: NBA cannot choose between variants it has never been told about, so the index feed is a first-class deliverable, not plumbing.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:position:3" class="decide">
    <h4><span class="stamp">Q3</span>One content reference everywhere; channel is a parameter, not a different ID</h4>
    <p>All channels quote the same reference. The channel is passed alongside it, and selects which channel rendering is returned. This makes both statements in the source document true at once: every surface is fetching the same content, and each surface gets copy shaped for it.</p>
    <p>It also gives the consistency test something to assert against — every response carries a <code>consistencyKey</code> derived from variant, version, locale and parameters, and identical keys across channels is a machine-checkable pass.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:position:5" class="decide">
    <h4><span class="stamp">Q6b</span>Tone belongs to the partner; register belongs to the surface</h4>
    <p>Tone — educational, familiar, data-led — is a <strong>selection criterion</strong>. NBA chooses it per partner from engagement history, and it stays constant as the partner moves from the email to the portal to a conversation with their Market Manager. That persistence is the benefit EG's own step 5 describes.</p>
    <p>Register is the opposite: length, formality, and who is being addressed. It is <strong>fixed per surface and identical for every partner</strong>, so it lives on the channel rendering and is never selected. Alongside it sits <code>audience</code> — partner or internal — because the CRM rendering does not address the partner at all. It briefs a Market Manager on what the partner has already seen, which is a different piece of writing with different review criteria.</p>
    <p>Channel therefore never enters variant selection. It is supplied on the fetch, picks a rendering, and acts as an availability filter on the index — a constraint, not a criterion. Section 03 sets the four dimensions out side by side.</p>
  </div>

</Section>


<Section n="02" id="model" title="Content model">
<div data-origin="uc1:model:*">
  <p class="sec-note">Four content types plus taxonomy. Variants are modelled as referenced entries rather than a variant dimension, because core entries have no such dimension — this is the hand-modelled route, and it is deliberate here rather than a fallback.</p>

  <div class="fig">
    <Fragment set:html={uc1Fragment1} />
  </div>
  <p class="cap">Figure 1 — Content model. The variant is the addressable unit: it is what NBA chooses and what the content reference points at.</p>

  <h3>Why the variant is the addressable unit</h3>
  <p>NBA's decision is "this segment, this tone" — so the thing it selects has to be the thing identified. Making the base proof point addressable would force a second resolution step somewhere, and the only candidates are Contentful (ruled out by Q2) or each channel independently (which is the drift problem the pilot exists to solve). Channel and locale are narrower: they don't change <em>which</em> message is being told, only how it is dressed, so they belong as parameters on the fetch rather than in the identity.</p>

  <h3>Approval state on the entry</h3>
  <p>The <code>approvals[]</code> field records who signed off on what and when — variant copy, per-locale translation, channel rendering. It exists because the audit requirement is about editorial accountability, not just field history: "who approved the Japanese version of the familiar-tone variant" is the question that gets asked, and version history alone answers it awkwardly.</p>
</div>
</Section>


<Section n="03" id="ref" title="The content reference">
<div data-origin="uc1:ref:*">
  <p class="sec-note">The identifier the whole use case rests on. It does not exist natively — the platform issues an ID per entry, with locale as a query parameter and version as a revision counter — so it has to be defined as a contract and implemented.</p>

  <Fragment set:html={uc1Fragment2} />

  <ul class="tight">
    <li><strong>The reference is opaque to channels and meaningful to us.</strong> SFMC passes it through without parsing; the resolution service decomposes it. Keeping it decomposable matters more than keeping it short — an opaque hash is untraceable at 2am.</li>
    <li><strong>Version is part of the identity, not metadata.</strong> Without it, a republished entry silently changes what a send delivers, and the traceability requirement ("given this ID, show me the exact content") becomes unanswerable for anything historical.</li>
    <li><strong>Locale and channel travel beside it, not inside it.</strong> That is what makes "the same content ID everywhere" literally true across channels, which is the claim the pilot is testing.</li>
    <li><strong>Parameters are the partner's data and never persist in the CMS.</strong> They exist in the decision payload and in the resolved response.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Segment, tone and channel are not the same kind of thing</h3>
  <p>The source document uses "channel-specific variant" in one place and "variant dimension" in another, which makes channel look like a peer of segment and tone. It isn't. The three sit in different halves of the system, and conflating them is the most expensive modelling mistake available here.</p>
  <div class="scroll">
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th style="width:16%">Dimension</th><th style="width:16%">Role</th><th style="width:22%">Decided by</th><th>What it changes</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td><strong>Segment</strong></td><td>Selection criterion</td><td>NBA, from partner attributes</td><td>Which message wins. Remove it and every partner receives identical copy — the personalisation thesis collapses.</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Tone</strong></td><td>Selection criterion</td><td>NBA, from engagement history</td><td>How the same point is framed for this partner — educational, familiar, data-led. A peer of segment; both are inputs to one decision, and it has to survive the move from one channel to the next.</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Register</strong></td><td>Property of the rendering</td><td>Inherited from the channel</td><td>Length, formality, and who is being addressed. Never selected and never varies by partner.</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Channel</strong></td><td>Rendering parameter, and an availability constraint</td><td>The requester — it already knows</td><td>What shape the winning message arrives in. Remove it and nothing breaks logically; copy simply doesn't fit its surface.</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Locale</strong></td><td>Rendering parameter</td><td>The partner record</td><td>Which translation of the winning message is served. Never selects between variants.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  </div>
  <p>Tone and register are the pair most easily conflated, and the source document uses the word "tone" for both. They pull in opposite directions: tone is chosen per partner and must stay constant across surfaces, while register is fixed per surface and identical for every partner. Keeping them as one field would mean a partner's voice changing every time they move between the email, the portal and a conversation with their Market Manager.</p>
  <p>Register carries a second thing the source document implies without stating: the CRM rendering does not address the partner at all. It briefs a Market Manager on what the partner has already seen, so it has to carry the partner's copy <em>and</em> be usable as a talking point. That is why <code>audience</code> sits on the rendering as <code>partner</code> or <code>internal</code> — email and VMC are partner-facing, CRM is not, and generation prompts and review criteria differ accordingly.</p>
  <p>Channel constrains without being a criterion: if a variant has no email rendering, NBA shouldn't dispatch it to email — but that absence never makes a different variant a better fit for the partner. It is a filter on the index, not an input to the choice.</p>
  <p>Channel still earns its place in the pilot, for a different reason. Without it, cross-channel consistency is trivially true — one string fetched three times proves nothing. With it, the claim becomes that one authored source yields three differently-shaped outputs carrying the same message, which is CMS-01 and CMS-06 tested together. That is why <code>consistencyKey</code> covers variant, version, locale and parameters but deliberately excludes the channel shaping.</p>
</div>
</Section>


<Section n="04" id="authoring" title="Authoring flow">
<div data-origin="uc1:authoring:*">
  <p class="sec-note">Four phases, three human gates. Generation is automated; approval never is. Nothing reaches a channel without a named person having accepted it.</p>

  <div class="fig">
    <Fragment set:html={uc1Fragment3} />
  </div>
  <p class="cap">Figure 2 — Authoring flow. The design decision worth arguing about is in phase C.</p>

  <h3>Phase B — generated tone and channel variants</h3>
  <p>An AI action runs against the approved base template and produces a draft variant per tone, then a draft channel rendering per channel with that channel's constraints applied. The author sees a diff and accepts, edits or rejects. Two things make this safe enough to put in front of a client:</p>
  <ul class="tight">
    <li><strong>Placeholders are validated, not trusted.</strong> Generated copy is rejected automatically if it drops, renames or duplicates a parameter from <code>parameterSchema</code>. A tone rewrite that loses <code>\{views\}</code> is a broken send, and it is the most likely generation failure.</li>
    <li><strong>Provenance is recorded.</strong> <code>generatedBy</code> and the approving user are stored on the entry, so any later review can separate machine-written copy from hand-written copy.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Availability of the AI actions feature on EG's plan needs confirming before this is committed. If it isn't available, the same flow runs through an external generation step writing drafts via the management API — more moving parts, same gates.</p>

  <h3>Phase C — the translation approval gate</h3>
  <p>This is where a platform constraint bites: publishing acts on the entry, so every locale goes live together, and a per-locale approval gate appears impossible.</p>
  <p>The way through is to treat the empty field as the gate. A translation lives in the translation workflow until a reviewer approves it, and only then is it written into the localised field. Unapproved locales stay empty, native fallback serves the source copy, and the resolver flags that a fallback happened. Publishing becomes safe at any moment because the entry never contains unapproved copy.</p>
  <p>The trade is that an unapproved locale is not "blocked" — it is served in English and reported as such. For a pilot testing whether partners get consistent content that is the better failure mode, and it turns a modelling problem into a reporting one. It should be stated explicitly to EG rather than discovered at the readout.</p>
</div>
</Section>


<Section n="05" id="validation" title="Validation plan">
<div data-origin="uc1:validation:*">
  <p class="sec-note">The seven points you listed, each with the evidence it produces and the condition that decides pass or fail. Every one is machine-checkable except the two where the judgement is genuinely editorial.</p>
  <div class="scroll">
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th style="width:20%">Validation point</th><th style="width:34%">How it's exercised</th><th>Pass condition</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Variants of the same content</strong></td>
        <td>Two proof points, three tones, two segments — twelve variants. Each fetched by its own reference and diffed against the others.</td>
        <td>Twelve variants resolve independently and correctly from two base proof points, with no duplicated base copy. Adding a thirteenth needs no content-type change.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Automatic generation for tone and channel</strong></td>
        <td>AI action generates tone variants from the approved base, then channel renderings from each variant — applying that channel's register, audience and constraints — into draft state.</td>
        <td>Generated copy preserves every parameter in <code>parameterSchema</code>; the placeholder check rejects any output that drops or renames one; the partner tone survives into every channel rendering while the register changes with the surface; the author can accept, edit or reject without leaving the editor; provenance is recorded on the entry.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Translation into five locales</strong></td>
        <td>Approved variants sent for translation into the four target locales; three returned and approved; one deliberately held back untranslated.</td>
        <td>Translated copy keeps every variable intact and correctly placed for the target language; the untranslated locale serves source copy with <code>fallbackApplied: true</code>; no other locale is affected; Arabic round-trips without corruption.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Approval workflows</strong></td>
        <td>Three gates exercised end to end — generated copy, per-locale translation, release — including one rejection at each gate.</td>
        <td>Nothing unapproved is ever retrievable through the resolution API; a rejection returns the item to draft without affecting published content; each approval records who and when.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Version registry</strong></td>
        <td>A published variant is fetched, then edited and republished, then fetched again by the original reference.</td>
        <td>The original reference returns the original copy, byte for byte. The new reference returns the new copy. Both are retrievable, and the diff between versions is inspectable with author and timestamp.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>SFMC integration</strong></td>
        <td>SFMC fetches by reference at send time against the future-state instance and renders into a preview or seed send.</td>
        <td>Content populates the message from a single call; timeout, unknown reference and retired version each produce a defined, non-breaking behaviour; measured latency and throughput ceiling are documented rather than assumed.</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>API returns the published version by reference, variant and locale</strong></td>
        <td>The same reference requested across three channels and five locales, repeatedly, including after a republish.</td>
        <td><code>consistencyKey</code> is identical across channels for a given reference, version, locale and parameter set; repeated fetches are byte-identical; the response always states which locale was actually served.</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  </div>
  <p>Two judgements stay human and should be scheduled as such: whether a generated tone variant is <em>good</em>, and whether the translated copy reads naturally in each locale. Both need a named reviewer per locale, and neither is a test we can run on EG's behalf. Reviewers should be briefed that grammatical agreement with the variable is out of scope for this round, so they flag translation quality rather than re-reporting a known limitation.</p>
</div>
</Section>


<Section n="06" id="alternatives" title="Alternatives considered">

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:0" class="decide risk">
    <h4>Tone and channel as a single dimension</h4>
    <p>The argument for it is real: the channel does determine the voice of a communication, so a separate tone dimension could look redundant. Collapsing the two would simplify the model and cut the variant count by roughly a third.</p>
    <p>Not adopted, because it works against the source document's thesis rather than simplifying it. Step 5 states that the CRM shows the Market Manager the same tone of voice the partner already received, and the partner walkthrough presents persistent, persona-matched framing as the benefit being tested. If tone is a property of the channel, a partner's voice changes every time they move between the email, the portal and a call — the drift the pilot exists to eliminate.</p>
    <p>It would also cost three capability results at once: CMS-19 loses half its pass condition because tone stops being decisioning metadata, CMS-31's criterion collapses from segment plus tone to segment alone, and CMS-06 becomes trivially satisfiable since no cross-channel constant remains.</p>
    <p>The observation underneath it is kept: channel does carry a voice, and that voice is modelled as <code>register</code> on the rendering, alongside <code>audience</code>. Should EG's operating model genuinely treat tone as channel-determined, the change is one field plus a re-scoping of those three capabilities — an explicit client decision, which is why it is raised as a question rather than assumed either way.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:2" class="decide risk">
    <h4>One entry per partner, holding the partner's metric</h4>
    <p>The literal reading of the scale note. Not taken: it puts partner data in a content store, makes the entry count a function of the partner base, and makes every metric refresh a content operation. Nothing about it is a content-management problem.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:3" class="decide risk">
    <h4>Contentful resolving segment and tone to a variant</h4>
    <p>Fewer round trips and closer to how personalisation products work. Not taken because it contradicts the stated parameter that NBA decides, pulls a licensed personalisation capability onto the critical path, and makes the fetch non-deterministic and harder to cache. Worth revisiting after the pilot if EG wants decisioning closer to the content.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-origin="uc1:alternatives:4" class="decide risk">
    <h4>An opaque hashed content ID</h4>
    <p>Tidier on the wire. Not taken: an opaque token cannot be read in a log, a support ticket or an SFMC preview, and the traceability requirement is precisely about being able to answer "what was this" long after the send.</p>
  </div>
</Section>
